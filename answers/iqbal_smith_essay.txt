[HEADING] Muhammad Iqbal, Adam Smith and the Modern World Economy

Muhammad Iqbal and Adam Smith belonged to very different intellectual worlds. Smith was an eighteenth-century Scottish moral philosopher writing during the development of commercial society in Europe, while Iqbal was a twentieth-century Muslim philosopher writing under colonial rule and confronting industrial capitalism, imperialism and modern Western thought. Iqbal did not produce a systematic commentary on Adam Smith. Therefore, any comparison between them must be understood as a philosophical reconstruction rather than a claim that Iqbal directly answered Smith. Nevertheless, the comparison is highly productive because both thinkers were concerned with human freedom, moral agency, economic activity and the relationship between individual conduct and social institutions.

A modern epistemic reading asks a question deeper than whether Iqbal supported or opposed capitalism. It asks what each thinker believed could count as knowledge about human beings and society. From this perspective, Iqbal provides a powerful criticism of any interpretation of Smith that reduces human beings to purely self-interested economic actors. At the same time, a careful reading of Smith shows that Smith himself was considerably more morally sophisticated than the simplified version of him associated with unrestricted laissez-faire capitalism.

[HEADING] Iqbal’s epistemology

Iqbal’s starting point is that human knowledge cannot be reduced to one method. In The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam, he accepts the importance of reason, experience and scientific investigation, but rejects the idea that empirical science alone exhausts everything that can legitimately be known. Religious experience, moral consciousness and the active experience of the self also possess cognitive significance for him. In his first lecture, Iqbal explicitly treats religion, philosophy and higher poetry as different approaches to fundamental questions about reality and humanity.

This position is important economically. If the human person is understood only through measurable preferences, production, consumption and utility, then economic reasoning captures only part of human existence. For Iqbal, the individual is a developing moral self, or khudi, whose purpose is not merely the satisfaction of wants but the creative development of personality through action.

Iqbal therefore does not reject modern knowledge. His project is instead reconstructive. He seeks to bring Islamic thought into engagement with modern science and philosophy while refusing to accept the assumption that modern materialist categories provide a complete description of reality. His philosophy consequently challenges both intellectual traditionalism and uncritical imitation of Western modernity.

This epistemological position provides the foundation for his economic thought.

Iqbal had engaged economics long before the mature philosophical writings for which he became famous. His Ilm-ul-Iqtisad, written around 1903 and published in 1904, introduced modern economics to an Urdu-reading audience and drew upon the economic literature circulating at the time, including the classical tradition associated with Smith, Ricardo and Mill. Importantly, Iqbal was already concerned with poverty and the limits of economics as a self-contained discipline. Economic mechanisms alone, in his view, could not answer the moral question of what kind of social order human beings ought to create.

[HEADING] Adam Smith beyond simple self-interest

The popular image of Adam Smith often reduces his philosophy to the proposition that individuals pursuing their own economic interests automatically produce the best society. That interpretation is incomplete.

Smith wrote The Theory of Moral Sentiments before The Wealth of Nations. His moral philosophy begins with human beings as social creatures capable of sympathy, moral judgment and concern for how their conduct appears to an impartial spectator. Economic behaviour therefore exists within a wider moral and social order.

Smith certainly defended substantial economic freedom. He believed that individuals normally possessed better knowledge of their immediate economic circumstances than distant officials and strongly criticized mercantilist systems in which governments granted privileges to favoured economic interests. Yet Smith was also suspicious of merchants gaining political power, supported important public functions for government and treated justice as indispensable to a functioning commercial society.

This produces an interesting convergence with Iqbal.

Neither thinker can adequately be understood through the model of homo economicus, in which the individual is simply a machine for maximizing material advantage. Smith places economic self-interest within a moral psychology involving sympathy, justice and self-command. Iqbal goes further by placing economic activity within a metaphysical and spiritual conception of human personality.

Their disagreement therefore concerns not simply markets versus state control. It concerns the ultimate meaning of freedom and human flourishing.

[HEADING] Iqbal’s challenge to commercial individualism

Smith regarded commercial society as capable of increasing independence and prosperity. Market exchange allows individuals to cooperate without requiring personal submission to feudal masters. Smith therefore saw an important relationship between commerce and liberty.

Iqbal could accept part of this argument. He opposed passivity, dependency and structures that destroyed human initiative. Even his early economic writing connected freedom with productive motivation and self-respect.

However, Iqbal would object if economic freedom were defined merely as freedom to contract, accumulate property and pursue private advantage.

For Iqbal, genuine freedom requires the strengthening of human personality. An economic order that formally allows exchange but leaves large populations impoverished, dominated or incapable of meaningful self-development cannot represent complete freedom.

This explains his increasingly severe criticism of capitalism. His later poetry attacked concentrations of wealth, exploitation and the relationship between capitalist power and imperial domination. Scholarship on Iqbal has therefore interpreted his critique of Western capitalism not merely as an economic objection but as a moral and civilizational critique.

Iqbal was also critical of feudalism and extreme concentrations of landed power. His alternative cannot consequently be described simply as hostility toward markets or private initiative. He objected to economic arrangements when property and capital became instruments through which one part of society acquired domination over another.

[HEADING] The epistemic disagreement

The deepest difference can be expressed in terms of how economic knowledge is constructed.

A narrow modern economic interpretation might begin with observable individual preferences. Individuals choose according to their interests, prices communicate information and market outcomes emerge from decentralized interaction. The central analytical question then becomes how efficiently resources are allocated.

Iqbal would regard this as useful but incomplete.

Before asking how efficiently preferences are satisfied, he would ask how those preferences are formed, what conception of the human person they presuppose and whether satisfying them contributes to the development or degradation of the self.

This distinction is crucial.

Economic science can tell us that a particular allocation is efficient according to specified preferences. Efficiency alone cannot establish that those preferences are morally desirable.

Economic science can describe the distribution of income. It cannot, using descriptive economic reasoning alone, establish what distribution should be considered just.

Economic science can measure growth. Growth itself cannot determine whether a society produces greater human dignity, domination, spiritual development or alienation.

Iqbal therefore introduces a normative level of knowledge into political economy. Facts concerning production and exchange matter, but facts must be interpreted through a conception of the person and the good society.

Yet this should not be used to construct an artificial opposition between Iqbal and Smith. Smith himself did not regard economics as morally self-sufficient. His Theory of Moral Sentiments places commercial behaviour within an account of virtue, justice, prudence and social judgment. Modern scholarship consequently treats Smith's moral philosophy and political economy as elements of a wider intellectual project rather than two completely separate systems.

In this respect, an Iqbalian critique may apply more strongly to later reductions of Smith than to Smith himself.

[HEADING] Smith and the modern world economy

Smith's historical influence on the world economy has been enormous, but the relationship is indirect. The contemporary international economy was not simply designed according to The Wealth of Nations. Industrialization, colonialism, technological change, financial institutions, state policy, corporations and later economic theories all shaped the development of global capitalism.

Smith nevertheless helped establish some of the central intellectual foundations of modern political economy. His arguments against mercantilist privilege, his analysis of specialization and division of labour, his defence of relatively decentralized economic decision-making and his account of commercial society became major reference points for subsequent liberal economic thought.

The economic world influenced by this tradition has produced remarkable productive capacity and extensive international specialization. Yet Iqbal directs attention toward another side of the historical process.

The same modern world contained colonial domination, deep inequalities in political and economic power, extreme concentrations of wealth and the subordination of large populations to economic structures they did not control. Iqbal encountered modern capitalism not from eighteenth-century Scotland but from British India. The location from which he observed the system therefore mattered.

From an Iqbalian perspective, the correct measure of a global economic system cannot be aggregate wealth alone. One must ask what happens to human agency.

Does economic development strengthen the capacity of individuals and societies to determine their futures?

Does it reduce degrading poverty?

Does it preserve the dignity of labour?

Does wealth become concentrated to such an extent that economic power becomes political domination?

Does economic dependence weaken the autonomy of communities?

These questions transform the evaluation of the world economy from a purely quantitative exercise into an ethical and political one.

[HEADING] Iqbal, Smith and the problem of unintended consequences

There is another interesting connection between the two thinkers.

Smith repeatedly emphasized that complex social orders often emerge without being deliberately designed. Individuals acting from limited knowledge can unintentionally contribute to larger social outcomes. His political philosophy therefore contains a strong suspicion of rulers or intellectuals who imagine that society can simply be reorganized according to a comprehensive plan.

Iqbal also rejects a static conception of history. His philosophy emphasizes creativity, action and an unfinished world in which human beings participate in the formation of the future. The universe, for Iqbal, is not simply a fixed mechanism to be contemplated. Human action has genuine significance.

But Iqbal adds a different requirement. Spontaneous development does not automatically possess moral legitimacy merely because nobody centrally planned it. An institution produced through millions of decentralized actions can still generate injustice or human degradation.

This provides perhaps the strongest synthesis of Smith and Iqbal.

Smith warns against excessive confidence in centralized human reason. Iqbal warns against excessive confidence in material processes themselves.

Smith asks us not to assume that a planner knows enough to organize society.

Iqbal asks us not to assume that an economically successful spontaneous order is therefore morally sufficient.

[HEADING] Conclusion

Viewed through modern epistemology, the relationship between Muhammad Iqbal and Adam Smith is therefore more complex than a confrontation between Islam and capitalism.

Both thinkers defend human agency. Both recognize the importance of economic freedom. Both distrust systems that transform individuals into passive instruments of larger powers. Both also place economic life within a wider understanding of human society.

Their decisive difference lies in the ultimate standard by which economic arrangements are evaluated.

Smith's political economy emphasizes liberty, justice, decentralized knowledge and the productive possibilities of commercial society. Iqbal accepts the importance of empirical knowledge and productive activity but insists that economic life must remain subordinate to a richer conception of the human person. Knowledge of prices, production and preferences cannot substitute for knowledge concerning justice, dignity and the development of the self.

The modern world economy demonstrates why this distinction remains important. Smithian ideas contributed to an intellectual tradition that helped explain the extraordinary coordinating and productive capacities of markets. Yet the historical development of global capitalism also produced questions concerning inequality, concentrated power, colonialism and human purpose that cannot be answered by efficiency alone.

An Iqbalian reading therefore does not require rejecting Adam Smith. It requires recovering the moral dimension already present in Smith and carrying it further. Economic institutions must be judged not only according to what they produce, but according to what kind of human beings and social relationships they create.

The most significant epistemic lesson produced by placing the two thinkers together is consequently that political economy cannot be separated completely from moral philosophy. A world economy may be technically efficient while remaining ethically deficient. For Iqbal, the final purpose of economic organization would not merely be the accumulation of wealth, but the creation of conditions in which free, responsible and creative human personalities can develop.
