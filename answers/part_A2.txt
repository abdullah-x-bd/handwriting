Part A.2

Physiocracy was an important school of economic thought that developed in eighteenth-century France. Its leading thinkers included Francois Quesnay and A. R. J. Turgot. The Physiocrats believed that economic society was governed by a natural order and that economic policy should interfere as little as possible with this order.

Their most important belief was that agriculture was the only activity capable of producing a net surplus. They called this surplus the produit net. Manufacturing and trade were considered useful but "sterile" because, according to them, these activities merely transformed existing products rather than creating a new surplus.

The Physiocrats divided society into three main classes. The productive class consisted mainly of farmers. The proprietary class consisted of landowners. The sterile class consisted of manufacturers, artisans and merchants.

Quesnay explained the circulation of income among these classes through his Tableau Economique. It was one of the earliest attempts to explain the economy as an interconnected system of flows.

The Physiocrats also supported private property, free trade and laissez-faire. Although their claim that only agriculture is productive was later rejected, their ideas influenced Adam Smith and contributed to the development of theories of production, surplus and circular flow.
